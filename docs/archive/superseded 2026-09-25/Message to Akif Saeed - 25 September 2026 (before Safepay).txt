Assalam o Alaikum Sir,

Following your guidance on jurisdiction, I have revised the parts of the model
that created regulatory exposure. I wanted to put the findings in front of you
before I go further.

THE STRUCTURAL CHANGE

The earlier design had members compensating each other. A member taking an early
turn paid a fee that was distributed to the others, and positions could be sold
at a premium between members. That is a lender and a borrower in substance, and
it falls within the peer to peer definition in the NBFC Regulations,
which reads "the extension of loans by the lender to the borrower through the
P2P Lending Platform".

That is now removed. Every payment a member makes splits three ways at the moment
it is taken, and the three parts are never commingled:

  1. The contribution, which settles from the payer to the collecting member
     through a licensed electronic money institution. Halqa never touches it.
     The identity pot = contribution x periods still holds exactly, so paid in
     equals collected for every member.

  2. A takaful contribution, which sits in the participants' risk fund at a
     licensed operator.

  3. A service fee, flat across the whole roster, paid to Halqa.

The fee is deliberately flat rather than graded by collection day. A fee that
varies with how early a member collects is priced on the advance and reads as
interest. A flat fee for a service does not.

I am relying on the Explanation to section 84 of the Companies Act, which
excludes "an advance against sale of goods or provision of services in the
ordinary course of business" from the meaning of a deposit.

ON COVER

I had designed a cover facility funded by a fee and paying out on a member's
default. On reading section 2(xxvii) of the Insurance Ordinance I found that is
insurance "by whatever name called", and section 5(1) admits only a public
company, so a private limited company cannot be registered as an insurer at all.

Cover is therefore written and priced by a licensed general takaful operator,
since default cover is Class 6 credit and suretyship business. Halqa acts as
the operator's corporate insurance agent under a written agency agreement, as
section 96(2) of the Ordinance requires, and earns commission. The contribution
sits in the participants' risk fund, which belongs to the participants rather
than to the operator, surplus returns to them at the end of the period, and a
deficit is met by the operator from its own shareholder fund. Members are
protected in a mass default event without Halqa's balance sheet being the
backstop.

THE COLLAPSE THRESHOLD

I built a model to answer when a circle stops being able to pay what it owes.
Four results:

  Arrears before collection recover themselves. A member who misses payments
  before their turn has those arrears netted from their own pot. It is a timing
  gap, not a loss.

  The only true loss is default after collection. Forward liability is the
  contribution multiplied by the periods remaining. It is worst on day one and
  nil on the last day, and averages half a pot.

  Expected loss on a cycle is therefore the roster multiplied by the default
  rate multiplied by half a pot. As a share of cycle value that is simply half
  the default rate.

  The circle cannot complete once unrecovered exposure exceeds the cover limit.
  Before that point a five axis stress index moves the circle through green,
  amber and red, with a defined action at each band.

The cover is sized at a fifty per cent post collection default rate, more than
eight times the highest arrears rate reported for Pakistani microfinance, which
peaked at 6.01 per cent.

ON COLLECTION

Section 35(1) of the Payment Systems Act permits a preauthorised transfer to be
authorised "either in writing, or in any other accepted form", so an in app
mandate is valid, and section 35(2) lets the member stop it at any time. The
duties that attach to it, in sections 30, 31 and 36, fall on the member's bank
and on the licensed payment institution rather than on Halqa. The design
supports each of them.

The rail decides the structure. A Raast Request to Pay settles to the merchant
that sends it, so if Halqa sent one for a contribution the contribution would
land in Halqa's account. Halqa therefore uses a Request to Pay only for its own
fee. Contributions move from member to member through an electronic money
institution, which under the 2023 EMI Regulations may provide payment
initiation and aggregation (para 7.I(f)) and may provide those services to a
fintech through its interface (para 7.I(h)). A payment service provider cannot
do this, because the 2014 Rules provide that it "will not act as custodian of
consumer's money". The daily product needs no escrow: each day's payers pay
that day's collectors directly, so no pool exists at any point.

WHERE THIS LEAVES THE LICENSING QUESTION

On the restructured design I do not find an activity that requires a financial
licence. No deposits are taken, nothing is lent, no member is compensated by
another member, no balance is issued, no payment is routed or switched by
Halqa, no insurance is written and no credit bureau is run. Every movement of
member money is carried by a licensed institution under its own licence: the
electronic money institution where Halqa initiates the payment through its
interface, or the member's own bank where the member sends it over Raast.
What Halqa itself requires is incorporation, a tax number, sales tax
registration in Islamabad, a company account and the institution's agreement,
none of which carries a capital requirement.

I read the sandbox as the route to a No Objection Letter where an activity needs
authorisation and no enabling framework exists. Here the enabling framework is
para 7.I(h) of the EMI Regulations, and the institution notifies the State Bank
thirty days before it serves us. So my intention is to proceed under the general
law. I would value your view on whether that reading is right, because the plan
depends on it.

FOUR THINGS I WOULD ASK

  Whether the three way split at source, with the fee flat across the roster,
  is enough to keep the arrangement outside the peer to peer chapter in
  substance and not only in form.

  Whether the State Bank would regard a fintech that arranges member to member
  collection through a licensed EMI's interface, under para 7.I(h), as needing
  anything of its own beyond that institution's notification.

  Whether a written agency agreement with a general takaful operator is the
  correct instrument for distributing its cover alongside a committee, given that
  regulation 10(c) of the 2020 Regulations bars coercing a prospect to buy
  cover, regulation 11(1)(d) requires the cost of a bundled product to be
  shown separately, and the daily product includes cover in every payment.

  Whether the Federal Government notification contemplated by section 11(1) of
  the Credit Bureaus Act, for a furnisher that is not a credit institution, has
  been made for any category, or whether that remains closed in practice.
