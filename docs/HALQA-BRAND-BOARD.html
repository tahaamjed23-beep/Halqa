The mark

Ghareeb ka hisaab
ek copy mein likha jata hai.

The poor's financial system does not run on an app or a bank. It runs on a notebook — the organizer's register, a name in a column, a stroke against each month. Nine strokes for nine paid members. That register is the whole institution, and digitising it is the entire company.

So the mark is not a fintech circle. It is tally marks arranged in a ring — the register bent into the shape of the word halqa. Nine ivory strokes are the members who have paid. The tenth, gold and reaching into the centre, is whose turn it is now.

The Register Primary

Ten tally marks in a ring. The gold one is longer and points inward — the pot moving to this month's member. Reads as a circle, a clock, a register and a sunburst all at once. Nothing else in Pakistani fintech looks like it.

The Fold

A folded banknote — the physical currency of this economy — with the arc of the rotation rising from it. Warmer and more literal. Strong for print and merchant materials; slightly busier as an icon.

The Turn

The simplest possible statement: a ring, half of it gold, one member at the top. Maximum legibility, minimum meaning — the safe fallback if the register mark proves too fine for small print.

Scale test. 112px to 24px. The silhouette holds because it is nine identical strokes and one that breaks the pattern — the eye reads the break before it reads the detail.
Palette

Same theme. More voltage.

You liked the direction, so the pine and gold stay — but both are pushed harder. The green goes deeper so gold can sit on it at full strength, and the gold moves from a muted antique tone to one that survives a phone screen in Pakistani daylight. This is the difference between a heritage brochure and an app people tap.

Voice

Roman Urdu first.
English underneath.

JazzCash says "ab business karo, baithay baithay." Easypaisa says "dunya ko bataa do." Nobody in this market is won in formal English. The literature is blunt about why it matters: only 31% of committee users can read a text message, and women — who join at twice the rate of men — are the least served by dense English screens. Short Roman Urdu, big numbers, plain icons.
